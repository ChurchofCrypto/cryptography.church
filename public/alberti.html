The Alberti Disk

In 1467 Leon Battista Alberti described the first polyalphabetic cipher: a fixed ring of letters and a turning disk of mixed symbols. The coin is one. Step through a message below, or drag the disk yourself.

Nothing up our sleeve

Where the disk order comes from

Alberti mixed his disks by hand. Ours is a Fisher–Yates shuffle of a…z αβδζθξπσφψ, driven by SHA-256 of ALBERTI|0, ALBERTI|1, and so on: four bytes at a time, big-endian, with rejection sampling so no symbol is favoured. Anyone can recompute it, so we could not have picked a weak order on purpose.

The 26 lowercase letters get ten Greek letters instead of digits. As on Alberti's own disk, numbers live only on the fixed ring. No Greek glyph on the disk looks like a Latin one, upright or upside down, and the bars under b d n p q u mark their baseline so you can tell them apart in any rotation.

Faith, not security

This is 1467 technology, and the coin has no secret

The disk order is public: it is printed here and identical on every coin. The setting travels in clear. Anyone holding a coin can read your message.

Alberti's real security came from a disk order kept secret, and even that falls to frequency analysis once a message is long enough. For real secrets, use modern end-to-end encryption. Mathematics is the sacrament; the protocol is what protects you.
