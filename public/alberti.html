The Alberti Disk

In 1467 Leon Battista Alberti described the first polyalphabetic cipher: a fixed ring of letters and a turning disk of mixed symbols. The coin is one. Step through a message below, or drag the disk yourself.

Nothing up our sleeve

Where the disk order comes from

Alberti mixed his disks by hand. Ours is a Fisher–Yates shuffle of a…z αβδζθξπσφψ, driven by SHA-256 of ALBERTI|0, ALBERTI|1, and so on: four bytes at a time, big-endian, with rejection sampling so no symbol is favoured. Anyone can recompute it, so we could not have picked a weak order on purpose.

The 26 lowercase letters get ten Greek letters instead of digits. As on Alberti's own disk, numbers live only on the fixed ring. No Greek glyph on the disk looks like a Latin one, upright or upside down, and the bars under b d n p q u mark their baseline so you can tell them apart in any rotation.

Make one from paper

Print, cut, pin

No coin? Print two pages, cut out a plate and a disk, and push a brass paper fastener through both centres. Glue them to thin card first if you have some.

Page two has two disks. The coin's disk uses the same order as every coin, so your paper messages work with coin holders. The blank disk is for your own secret order: write the 36 symbols in any mixed order and give the only other copy to your correspondent. That is how Alberti meant it to be used, and it is the only version with any secret in it.

Faith, not security

Why a disk like this cannot keep a secret

The coin's key is public

Kerckhoffs's principle (1883): a cipher must stay safe when the enemy knows everything except the key. The coin's disk order is printed on every coin and on this page, and the setting is written in clear. Nothing secret is left, so anyone with a coin can read your message.

A secret disk only slows people down

Between two settings the disk is a simple substitution: one letter, one symbol. Count the symbols, match the commonest to E, T, A, O (or E, N, I, R in German) and the rest follows. The settings in clear even tell an attacker that every stretch is a turn of the same disk, so what they learn in one stretch unlocks all the others.

Nothing proves who wrote it

There is no signature and no checksum. Anyone holding the disk can forge a message in your name, and a changed symbol goes unnoticed. Modern ciphers authenticate every message, so tampering is detected.

The key has to travel by hand

You must meet to share a disk, and the same disk protects every message you ever send. Lose it once and all past and future messages are open. Modern protocols agree fresh keys over the network and throw old ones away.

Where to put your faith

Alberti's lasting lesson is that security lives in the key and in how you share it, never in the device. Today that faith rests on published mathematics, reviewed in the open and fixed in standards. Trust the standard, use a maintained implementation of it, and don't build your own.

The foundations

  • Auguste Kerckhoffs, “La cryptographie militaire”, 1883. Only the key may be secret.
  • Claude Shannon, “Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems”, Bell System Technical Journal, 1949. Secrecy as mathematics.
  • Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, “New Directions in Cryptography”, 1976. Agreeing a key in public, which ends the need to meet and hand over a disk.

The systems built on it

Private messages

Signal Protocol: PQXDH key agreement and the Double Ratchet

Fresh keys for every message, so a stolen phone does not expose the past. Verify safety numbers in person: the modern handing-over of the disk.

Implemented in: libsignal (the Signal app)

Group messages

Messaging Layer Security, RFC 9420, built on HPKE (RFC 9180)

The IETF standard for end-to-end encrypted groups of any size.

Implemented in: OpenMLS, MLSpp

Our Matrix room

Olm and Megolm, in the Matrix specification

Olm is a Double Ratchet; Megolm shares room keys. Verify sessions by comparing emoji.

Implemented in: vodozemac (Element and other clients)

The web

TLS 1.3, RFC 8446

The padlock in your browser.

Implemented in: OpenSSL, BoringSSL, rustls

Tunnels

Noise Protocol Framework; WireGuard (NDSS 2017)

A small handshake you can read in an afternoon.

Implemented in: WireGuard

Remote shells

SSH, RFC 4253, with curve25519-sha256 (RFC 8731) and Ed25519 keys (RFC 8709)

Implemented in: OpenSSH

Files

age v1 (C2SP)

Small and hard to misuse.

Implemented in: age, rage

Email and signed releases

OpenPGP, RFC 9580

Implemented in: Sequoia PGP; GnuPG implements the earlier RFC 4880
